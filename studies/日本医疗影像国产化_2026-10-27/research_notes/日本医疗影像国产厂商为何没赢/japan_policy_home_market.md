# Japan: government policy toward domestic CT/MRI makers, and how the home market evolved (1970s–2026)

Research date: 2026-10-27. Scope: Japan only. Each finding is tagged with its source type: [Primary] = government, statute or company document; [Academic] = peer-reviewed or working paper; [Trade press] = contemporaneous industry journalism; [Commentary] = opinion or secondary journalism; [Search-summary only] = seen only in a search-engine excerpt because the page itself could not be fetched (403, DNS failure or paywall). Search-summary-only items are weaker and are flagged.

Short answer to the hypothesis "the state backed domestic makers and they attacked the market with low prices":
- **Half right, but the mechanism is the reverse of China's VBP.** I found no evidence of a MITI-style supply-side program (subsidies, national R&D project) aimed at CT/MRI. The state's real lever was demand-side. MHW set a very low per-scan CT fee, cut it by more than half in 1978, and imposed no US-style certificate-of-need control. The result was mass demand from small private hospitals for cheap scanners, and domestic makers (Hitachi, Toshiba, Shimadzu) supplied it. Public hospitals (about 30% of device purchases in 1994) were also accused by the US of favoring domestic suppliers.
- **Japan never ran 集采/VBP for imaging equipment.** Equipment prices are not set by the state. The state sets the per-exam fee in the 診療報酬 schedule.
- **The repo note "Japan gave up on domestic substitution in high-end devices: CT/MRI long dependent on GE/Siemens" is false for CT and mostly false for MRI.** Domestic makers have led Japanese CT since about 1979, and Toshiba (now Canon) is #1 to this day. It is true for therapeutic devices such as pacemakers.

## 1. Industrial policy: did MITI/METI or MHW/MHLW give targeted support to domestic CT/MRI makers (1970s–1990s)?

### Takeaway
I found no evidence of a targeted MITI/METI program for CT or MRI comparable to the VLSI/computer projects. Toshiba and Hitachi developed MRI in-house in 1981–84. The health ministry treated devices as a safety and regulatory matter, not an industrial-policy target. Explicit cross-ministry device industrial policy only appears from 2007 onward. The state's real influence was demand-side, through the fee schedule (Section 2), and through public-hospital procurement that the US accused of favoritism (Section 3).

### Cited Findings
- Toshiba's own sequence for MRI:
  - October 1981: a clinical-MRI productization project launched in its medical-equipment division (Nasu works) together with its central research lab.
  - 1982: first domestic clinical trial, at Toshiba Central Hospital.
  - 1983: first domestically made MRI sold.
  - No government program is mentioned. [Search-summary only] — [Tohoku Univ. lecture PDF](https://www.med.tohoku.ac.jp/mov/llect/doc/220107my.pdf); [JIRA Radiology Japan](https://jira-net.or.jp/radiology_japan/rj_059/04.html)
- Hitachi's MRI history:
  - 1984: first MRI delivered, a resistive 0.15T "G-10" at Tokyo Women's Medical University.
  - 1985–86: started permanent-magnet MRI development. MRP-20 (0.2T) followed in 1987.
  - 1995: launched AIRIS, the first open MRI (0.3T).
  - Cumulative shipments: about 800 units by 1993, 2,200 by 1999, 2,500 by 2003, and over 5,000 by September 2008.
  - The company history credits in-house development and the arrival of neodymium magnets. It does not mention any state program. [Primary, company-sponsored] — [INNERVISION 2009 Hitachi Medical supplement](https://www.innervision.co.jp/01inner/2009/pdf/iv0904_hitachi01.pdf)
- Yokogawa Electric and GE:
  - December 1976: Yokogawa set up a CT division. From 1977 it sold GE's CT scanner in Japan.
  - 1982: the business became a GE joint venture, Yokogawa Medical Systems (YMS).
  - 2009: renamed GE Healthcare Japan.
  - So "GE" in Japan has manufactured in Japan, at Hino, since the 1980s. [Primary, company] — [Yokogawa centennial booklet](https://yokogawa.com/ebooks/100th_e/pageindices/index45.html); [JETRO success story](https://www.jetro.go.jp/en/invest/investment_environment/success_stories/gehealthcare.html)
- A 2011 paper argues the health ministry (MHW/MHLW) treated devices as a patient-safety matter rather than an industrial one. It applied the Pharmaceutical Affairs Law strictly and approvals were slow, so leading Japanese firms avoided medical devices (especially therapeutic ones). [Academic; Search-summary only] — [Tsutsumi 2011](https://wydawnictwo.not.pl/2011JOM/V3/6_TSUTSUMI.pdf)
- A joint MEXT/MHLW/METI device-industry strategy only dates from 2007. A 2016 METI presentation centers on development support and overseas deployment, not a long-running MITI program like electronics. [Search-summary only] — [MHLW industry vision material](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/bunya/iryou/shinkou/dl/03.pdf); [JEF 164th](https://www.jef.or.jp/journal/pdf/164th_cover06.pdf)
- A 2025 JEF analysis attributes Japan's weakness in therapeutic devices partly to corporate caution. Its example: Matsushita's stated fear that a device failure could hurt its home-appliance brand. [Commentary; Search-summary only; 403 on fetch] — [JEF 260th Cover Story 06](https://jef.or.jp/journal/pdf/260th_Cover_Story_06.pdf)
- Welfare-device R&D got a statute in 1993: the 福祉用具の研究開発及び普及の促進に関する法律, with NEDO subsidies. This covers welfare aids, not CT/MRI. [Primary; Search-summary only] — [Cabinet Office white paper 2002](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2002/zenbun/html/g3251200.html)
- In 1994, about 30% of Japan's device market was direct government purchasing. US officials argued that public-hospital choices shaped private purchases, because doctors trained on a given brand keep using it. [Trade press] — [Diagnostic Imaging, 1994-08-31](https://www.diagnosticimaging.com/view/us-charges-japan-unfair-practices-medical-device-buying)

### Inferences
- Supply side: the evidence points to in-house corporate R&D in vertically integrated electrical groups (Toshiba, Hitachi), not a MITI national project. This contrasts with the MITI VLSI project (1976–80) for semiconductors. Pre-1990 Japanese-language MITI records (通商産業政策史) were not searched in depth, so a small MITI program cannot be excluded.
- "State backing" in Japan's imaging story mostly means three things:
  - a low, uncapped-volume fee schedule that created mass demand;
  - a public procurement system the US alleged was opaque and domestic-leaning;
  - no CON-style limit on purchases.
  None of these is a subsidy to makers.

### Gaps
- 医療福祉機器技術研究開発制度 (reportedly a MITI/AIST medical-welfare device R&D scheme from 1976) and 技術研究組合医療福祉機器研究所: I could not find any document linking either to CT or MRI development. Status: UNVERIFIED.
- No budget figures for any state CT/MRI R&D support were found.
- Okimoto, *Between MITI and the Market* (1989), the standard work, was not accessible.

## 2. Procurement and pricing: was there ever VBP for imaging? How do hospitals buy? How did 診療報酬 shape demand? Why the world's highest CT/MRI density?

### Takeaway
I found no evidence of volume-based centralized procurement (VBP) of CT/MRI in Japan. The closest thing is a small joint tender run by the National Hospital Organization (国立病院機構), with other public hospital groups, of about 15–22 CTs and MRIs a year. That is a tiny fraction of an installed base in the tens of thousands, and it is ordinary competitive tendering, not price-setting. The state controls the price of the scan, not the machine.

The very low CT fees of the 1970s–80s, with no purchase controls, produced mass adoption by small private hospitals. That favored low-cost scanners, which domestic makers supplied. Since about 2008 the fee is tiered by machine performance and the low tiers have been cut, so the schedule now rewards higher-spec machines rather than pushing hospitals toward cheap ones. Japan has the most CT and MRI units per head in the OECD but the fewest exams per unit.

### Cited Findings
**Fee schedule and the 1970s–80s CT boom**
- 1973–78: Japan's CT fee was set at less than half the cost of a typical US scan. In 1978 the government cut the per-scan fee by more than half.
- Japan had no certificate-of-need (CON) style purchase restriction.
- The low fees led manufacturers to offer low-cost machines that were popular with small facilities, some without a board-certified radiologist.
- CT installations grew three times faster in Japan than in the US.
- [Academic] — [Bhidé, Datar & Stebbins, HBS Working Paper 20-004, "Computed Tomography (CT)" (©2020, 2024), citing Niki 1985](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/20-004_5ae5a400-203b-47ef-89a8-c2b6e7987f5c.pdf)
- Niki (1985), *Social Science & Medicine* 21(10):1131–37:
  - 2,120 CT scanners in Japan by December 1982, more than all European countries combined, and more per million people than the US.
  - Causes cited: no explicit government policy controlling CT adoption, spread to smaller private hospitals, and Japanese manufacturers' low-price CTs plus active marketing.
  - [Academic] — [PubMed 4081816](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4081816/)
- Niki (1983), *Shizen* 38(5):
  - The first CT scanner cost about ¥200 million.
  - Japan's CT fee was about one-fifth of the US fee.
  - 2,120 units by December 1982, about 18.5 per million, the highest in the world.
  - Installations grew about 20-fold from 1977 to 1981.
  - From 1979 domestic makers outperformed foreign products in Japan, led by Hitachi and Toshiba.
  - [Academic] — [OSTI 6501204](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/6501204)
- 2002 fee revision: both CT and MRI fees were cut, MRI by more. [Search-summary only] — [MHLW research grant report 2015](https://mhlw-grants.niph.go.jp/system/files/2015/151012/201502012A_upload/201502012A0014.pdf)

**2016 (H28) revision, per 中医協 総-1, 2019-10-23**
- CT:
  - 64-slice and above: 1,000 points, or 1,020 if done at a shared-use (共同利用) facility.
  - 16–64 slice: 900 points.
  - 4–16 slice: cut from 770 to 750.
  - Other: cut from 580 to 560.
- MRI:
  - 3T and above: 1,600 points, or 1,620 if shared-use.
  - 1.5–3T: 1,330 points.
  - Other: cut from 920 to 900.
- In other words, the low-end tiers were cut while the high-end tiers kept or gained points.
- [Primary] — [MHLW 中医協 総-1 (令和元年10月23日)](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12404000/000559332.pdf)
- Only 6% of surveyed MRI-owning facilities had billed any shared-use exams. 38% of clinics with CT did fewer than 30 CT exams a month. [Trade press, reporting the 中医協 meeting] — [日本医事新報](https://www.jmedj.co.jp/journal/paper/detail.php?id=13328)

**Unit density and usage**
- Per OECD Health Statistics 2015 (2013 or latest), as reproduced by MHLW in 2019:
  - Japan CT: 101.3 per million, versus Australia 53.7 and the US 43.5.
  - Japan MRI: 46.9 per million, versus the US 35.5.
  - MHLW also notes that CT and MRI exams per unit in Japan are the lowest among developed countries. Exams per head are the highest for CT and second (after Germany) for MRI.
  - [Primary] — [MHLW 中医協 総-1, 2019-10-23](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12404000/000559332.pdf)
- Japan CT: 115.7 per million in 2020 (OECD data via an aggregator). [Secondary] — [TheGlobalEconomy](https://www.theglobaleconomy.com/Japan/computed_tomography_scanners_per_million_people/)
- OECD Health at a Glance: Asia/Pacific 2024 gives Japan about 57 MRI per million and over 115 CT per million. [Search-summary only; 403 on fetch] — [OECD HaG Asia/Pacific 2024](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-asia-pacific-2024_51fed7e9-en/full-report/medical-technologies_39f58008.html)
- The OECD says Japan has by far the most CT and MRI units in the OECD (Health at a Glance 2023/2025). [Primary; exact figures not retrieved, 403] — [OECD HaG 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_8f9e3f98-en/full-report/diagnostic-technologies_9039027d.html); [AuntMinnieEurope, 2023-06-26](https://www.auntminnieeurope.com/clinical-news/article/15657971/global-report-pinpoints-key-trends-in-ct-and-mri-systems)

**Policy response and industry view**
- The imaging industry association JIRA's 2020 plan says:
  - The Finance Ministry has criticized the large number of CT/MRI units, and regional health planning (地域医療構想) is pushing "appropriate placement" (適正配置) and shared use.
  - The average service life of major equipment exceeds 12 years.
  - The industry lobbies for tax incentives, such as special depreciation, to spur replacement purchases.
  - [Primary, industry association] — [JIRA 2020 経済部会 事業計画](https://www.jira-net.or.jp/commission/files/2020_keizai.pdf)

**How hospitals buy**
- 国立病院機構 (National Hospital Organization) joint tender (共同入札) run with 労働者健康安全機構, JCHO and the Japanese Red Cross:
  - FY2026 plan (notice dated 2026-02-03): 22 multislice CTs, 22 MRIs and 14 angiography systems across 10 product types.
  - FY2024: 14 CTs and 18 MRIs.
  - FY2022: 20 CTs and 21 MRIs.
  - The notice is published under the WTO government-procurement listing on JETRO.
  - [Primary; Search-summary only] — [JETRO procurement notice 2026-02-03](https://www.jetro.go.jp/gov_procurement/national/articles/381174/2026020300580000.html); [NHO FY2024](https://nho.hosp.go.jp/news/nw1-1_00720.html); [NHO FY2022](https://nho.hosp.go.jp/news/nw1-1_00405.html)

### Inferences
- Japan's model is "regulate the service price, let equipment prices float." That is structurally the opposite of China's VBP, which sets or drives down the equipment price through volume tenders.
- Low per-scan fees plus no purchase controls meant that profitability for small hospitals depended on cheap capital equipment. This favored local makers with low-cost designs and dense domestic sales and service networks in the 1975–1990 period.
- From the 2000s, fee tiering by performance (slice count, tesla) favors upgrading. That is not a push toward cheap domestic machines; if anything, it rewards high-end units, where GE, Siemens and Philips compete hardest.
- The NHO joint tender (about 20 CTs a year) is far too small to set national equipment prices. Most purchases are individual hospital decisions, often through dealers.

### Gaps
- I did not find a clean year-by-year table of CT/MRI 撮影料 points (1978 to 2026). 中医協 archives would have it.
- No data were found on actual equipment purchase prices in NHO tenders, or on discount levels.
- No evidence was found on whether fee cuts in particular years shifted purchases toward domestic brands.
- Exact OECD values for 2022–2023 were not retrieved; OECD pages returned 403.

## 3. Trade policy: MOSS 1985–86, the 1994 procurement agreement, and the device trade deficit

### Takeaway
MOSS (agreed January 1986) was mainly about regulatory access:
- acceptance of foreign clinical data (with exceptions);
- faster and more transparent approvals;
- foreign input on reimbursement-pricing rules.
It was not about imaging market share. US firms themselves reported limited market-access gains. The sharper imaging-specific fight came in 1994, when the US threatened Title VII action over public-hospital procurement and Japan signed procurement "Measures" for medical technology. Japan's device trade deficit is concentrated in therapeutic devices. Imaging, where domestic makers are strong, is not the core of the deficit.

### Cited Findings
**MOSS (1985–86)**
- The agreement is dated January 9, 1986.
- Japan agreed to accept foreign clinical test data except where immunological or ethnic differences exist. For devices, the exceptions were implantables and devices affecting organic adaptability.
- MHW committed to announce its general rules for setting and revising device and IVD prices, with foreign industry able to comment.
- [Primary, USG; Search-summary only — site DNS failed at fetch] — [US Commerce TCC, MOSS agreement guide](https://tcc.export.gov/Trade_Agreements/All_Trade_Agreements/Report_MOSS_AG_guide.asp)
- A CIA assessment says the Health Ministry agreed to end medical-insurance practices that discriminated against US products. [Primary; Search-summary only] — [CIA reading room](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp04t00447r000201330001-5)
- GAO's 1987 interim report found that only 3 of 19 responding firms said MOSS increased their market access to a moderate or greater extent. Most valued fairer treatment of applications more. [Primary; Search-summary only] — [GAO NSIAD-87-186BR](https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad-87-186br)
- GAO's February 23, 1988 fact sheet to Sen. Bentsen surveyed 42 medical/pharma firms. It says post-MOSS data covered only the first half of Japan's FY1986, too early to judge. [Primary] — [GAO NSIAD-88-120FS](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-88-120fs.pdf)
- Congressional resolutions in 2004–05 still urged Japan to honor its 1986 MOSS commitments, focused on reimbursement of medical technology. [Primary] — [H.Con.Res.324 (108th)](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-108hconres324ih); [S.Con.Res.67 (109th)](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-109sconres67is)

**1994 public-procurement dispute and agreement**
- On August 31, 1994, USTR Mickey Kantor charged that Japan discriminated against US medical and telecom suppliers in government procurement, and set a 60-day Title VII deadline. The article also reported:
  - US makers held about 20% of Japan's medical device market, versus almost 40% in the EU.
  - Imaging was about 15% of Japan's device market.
  - Government purchases were about 30% of the device market.
  - US pacemaker makers held about 90% of Japan's pacemaker market.
  - Toshiba, Hitachi and Shimadzu were named as internationally competitive Japanese imaging suppliers, and GE YMS sales counted as US.
  - [Trade press] — [Diagnostic Imaging, 1994-08-31](https://www.diagnosticimaging.com/view/us-charges-japan-unfair-practices-medical-device-buying)
- In September 1994, Hitachi Medical took exclusive Japan distribution of Lorad (US) mammography systems. Hitachi expected sales of Japanese-made equipment to fall if the US talks succeeded. [Trade press] — [Diagnostic Imaging, 1994-09-28](https://diagnosticimaging.com/view/hitachi-builds-foreign-ties-us-japan-trade-deadline-looms)
- The agreement, "Measures Related to Japanese Public Sector Procurement of Medical Technology Products and Services," was an exchange of letters dated November 1, 1994 (one source says October 1). Its aim was to significantly increase foreign sales in public procurement, with annual reviews through FY2000. US negotiators had complained that Japanese suppliers learned of hospital procurements before public notice. [Primary; Search-summary only] — [US Commerce TCC guide](https://tcc.export.gov/Trade_Agreements/All_Trade_Agreements/Japan_Medical_Ag_guide.asp); [Kantei procurement report 2003](https://japan.kantei.go.jp/procurement/2003/ch/ch1-3.html)

**Trade balance**
- 2017 (MHLW 薬事工業生産動態統計 annual report, via JFMDA): device exports ¥619.0bn versus domestic shipments of imported products ¥1,649.2bn. [Primary-derived] — [JFMDA mini-column No.264](https://www.jfmda.gr.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/ミニコラム_20190614_第264号.pdf)
- 2015: domestic shipments about ¥2.72tn, exports about ¥0.62tn, production about ¥1.95tn, imports about ¥1.42tn. The source warns that this statistic is a production survey and not suited to measuring trade. [Secondary analysis] — [NLI Research](https://www.nli-research.co.jp/files/topics/56413_ext_18_0.pdf?site=nli)
- About 60% of Japan's device imports are therapeutic and related devices, and about 15% are diagnostic and related. [Search-summary only; attributed to a JFMDA compilation] — [NLI Research](https://www.nli-research.co.jp/files/topics/71477_ext_18_0.pdf?site=nli)
- 2024 (令和6年 annual report): production about ¥2.664tn, imports about ¥3.454tn (a five-year high), exports about ¥1.145tn, domestic shipments about ¥4.708tn. [Search-summary only — exact page among yakuji.co.jp results not confirmed; treat as UNVERIFIED] — [薬事日報 search results](https://www.yakuji.co.jp/?p=138047)

### Inferences
- MOSS was not an imaging-share event. In CT, domestic makers were already ahead of foreign products at home from 1979, before MOSS. MOSS mainly mattered for pharmaceuticals and implantables, where foreign clinical data and reimbursement pricing were the barriers.
- The 1994 procurement fight is the best documentary evidence for "state-favored domestic makers" in imaging. It is an allegation by the US (opaque tendering, insider notice), not proof of an official preference policy. Japan responded with transparency measures, not market-share guarantees.
- Japan's persistent device deficit is mostly therapeutic (catheters, implants, pacemakers). Imaging is the area where Japanese makers held up best. The "Japan depends on imports for high-end devices" framing fits therapeutic devices, not CT.

### Gaps
- No post-1994 data were found on the foreign share of Japanese public procurement of imaging equipment. The annual review reports exist but were not retrieved.
- No HS-code trade balance for CT (HS 9022.12) or MRI (HS 9018.13) was retrieved. Finance Ministry trade statistics would settle whether Japan runs a surplus in imaging.
- The full MOSS 1986 text and the medical-equipment export tables in GAO NSIAD-88-120FS were not readable (image tables).

## 4. Low-price competition: did Japanese makers compete mainly on price? What does the "HBS case" actually say?

### Takeaway
There is real evidence that Japanese makers sold low-cost CTs, at home in the late 1970s–80s and in world markets by the 1990s.

The "Harvard Business School case" is actually an HBS Working Paper (20-004), a "case history" by Bhidé, Datar and Stebbins. It says that by 1997 GE had lost global CT share to Toshiba and Shimadzu, both of which had introduced lower-cost CTs. It does not use the phrase "price war." No separate HBS case on Shimadzu or Yokogawa was found.

Price was not a one-way Japanese weapon. GE itself, through its Japanese JV YMS, launched a lower-priced 1.5T MRI exclusively for Japan in 1993 to attack Toshiba.

### Cited Findings
- HBS Working Paper 20-004, "Case Histories of Transformative Advances — Computed Tomography (CT): Beyond Traditional X-Rays," by Amar Bhidé, Srikant Datar and Katherine Stebbins (©2020, 2024). On the 1979–1997 period it says:
  - GE remained the global leader, helped by its 1982 Yokogawa JV in Japan, but had lost share to Siemens and Elscint (technology leaders) and to Toshiba and Shimadzu, "which had introduced lower-cost CTs."
  - GE held almost 33% of world CT sales in 1979.
  - Asia, where CT prices were sharply lower than elsewhere, grew faster than the US.
  - The market shares rest on Frost & Sullivan, "World X-ray and CT Equipment Markets" (January 1999), and OTA (1981).
  - The paper describes itself as summarizing history, not presenting original research.
  - [Academic] — [HBS WP 20-004](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/20-004_5ae5a400-203b-47ef-89a8-c2b6e7987f5c.pdf)
- The same paper's Japan section ties low prices to the fee schedule: low reimbursement encouraged manufacturers to offer low-cost machines for small facilities (citing Niki 1985). [Academic] — [HBS WP 20-004](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/20-004_5ae5a400-203b-47ef-89a8-c2b6e7987f5c.pdf)
- Niki (1985) lists "Japanese manufacturers have produced low-price CT" and active marketing among the causes of Japan's CT diffusion. [Academic] — [PubMed 4081816](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4081816/)
- In 1993, GE and YMS launched "Sierra," a 1.5T MRI sold only in Japan and priced below GE's Signa. It was aimed explicitly at Toshiba's 150A 1.5T system, and the article describes Toshiba as the leader of the Japanese MRI market. [Trade press; date inferred from content (shipping began January 1993)] — [Diagnostic Imaging, "GE targets Toshiba's Japanese MRI share"](https://diagnosticimaging.com/view/ge-targets-toshibas-japanese-mri-share)
- Hitachi built MRI volume on low-field permanent-magnet open systems (0.2–0.4T) from 1987 to the 2000s, and only launched a 1.5T superconducting system (ECHELON Vega) in 2006. [Primary, company-sponsored] — [INNERVISION 2009](https://www.innervision.co.jp/01inner/2009/pdf/iv0904_hitachi01.pdf)
- Shimadzu is reported as targeting a 1-tesla MRI niche, and a 1993-era cost-effective CT in the "$500,000 range." [Search-summary only; UNVERIFIED — page not fetched] — [Diagnostic Imaging, "Shimadzu targets 1-tesla MRI niche"](https://www.diagnosticimaging.com/view/shimadzu-targets-1-tesla-mri-niche)

### Inferences
- "Low price" was real but conditioned by Japan's demand structure: low fees and many small private hospitals. Japanese makers' cost-down designs (low-cost CT, low-field permanent-magnet MRI) were a rational fit to the home market. That is different from "state-subsidized dumping." No anti-dumping cases on Japanese CT/MRI were found.
- Globally, Toshiba's later rise to world #2 in CT (by its own data, 2016) came with multislice and Aquilion/320-row technology. Price was not the only lever. Inference only: Toshiba's tech claims were not fetched here.
- The claim "an HBS case named Shimadzu for low-cost CT / a price war" is partly accurate (low-cost CT, alongside Toshiba) but mislabeled. The document is a working paper, and it does not frame it as a price war.

### Gaps
- No classic HBS teaching case (e.g. "GE Medical Systems" or "Yokogawa Medical Systems") naming Shimadzu was found in the catalog searches. HBSP catalog pages were not directly searchable.
- No price data (yen per CT, by vendor, over time) beyond Niki's ¥200M launch price.

## 5. Home-market shares: who led Japanese CT and MRI over time? Test of the repo note

### Takeaway
**CT**
- Domestic makers led from about 1979: Hitachi and Toshiba (Niki 1983).
- Toshiba/Canon was #1 in Japanese CT through 2016, 2018 and 2024 by every source found.
- In the 2018 Fuji Chimera table, Hitachi (now Fujifilm) is listed second, ahead of Siemens, GE and Philips. GE is #2 per some vendor guides.

**MRI**
- Toshiba was described as the leader in 1993.
- Hitachi dominated low-field open-MRI unit volume.
- GE, Siemens and Philips are strong in high-field. No reliable recent vendor percentages were found.

**Verdict on the repo note** (studies/jp_cn_relative_timeline/zeitgeist_healthcare.md line 89, and automation_semis.md lines 19 and 113):
- "Japan gave up domestic substitution in high-end devices; CT/MRI long dependent on GE/Siemens" is **FALSE for CT**.
- It is **largely false or unproven for MRI**.
- It is **true for therapeutic devices** (pacemakers, catheters, implants).

### Cited Findings
- 1979 onward: domestic makers outperformed foreign products in Japan's CT market, led by Hitachi and Toshiba. [Academic] — [Niki 1983, OSTI 6501204](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/6501204)
